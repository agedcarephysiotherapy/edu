👋 Welcome to ACP

What this module covers

This induction is your starting point as a new Personal Care Assistant (PCA) working with Aged Care Physiotherapy Pty Ltd (ACP) and the facilities we support. It covers the compliance, safety and reporting knowledge every PCA needs from day one — duty of care, falls prevention, pressure injury prevention, risk and hazard management, and incident reporting.

This module is deliberately broad rather than deep. Falls prevention and manual handling have their own full clinical training modules — ask your supervisor for access once you've completed this induction. Treat this as the floor every PCA must clear, not the ceiling.

Your Role as a PCA

A PCA supports residents/clients with activities of daily living — showering, dressing, mobility, meals, continence care, and social engagement — under the direction of registered staff (RNs, ENs, physiotherapists, and other allied health). You are often the staff member who spends the most time with a resident, which makes you the person most likely to notice a change first.

  • You are not expected to diagnose, prescribe, or make clinical decisions.
  • You are expected to observe, report, and follow the care plan accurately.
  • When in doubt, escalate. No one has ever been criticised for reporting a concern that turned out to be nothing.
⚠ This training does not replace

Your organisation's policies and procedures, your facility orientation, or any accredited manual handling / infection control certification you're required to hold. Where they differ, your workplace policy always takes precedence over this module.

⚖️ Duty of Care & Scope of Practice

What duty of care means

Duty of care is your legal obligation to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm to the people you support. It applies to everything you do — physical care, supervision, environment, and communication.

Duty of Care

Taking reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that could foreseeably harm a resident. This includes acting on things you notice, not just things you're asked to do.

Dignity of Risk

Residents have the right to make their own choices and accept reasonable risks — for example, choosing to walk without a frame against advice. Your role is to inform, support safe choices, and document the discussion — not to override capacity or remove all risk.

Scope of Practice

The tasks you are trained, competent, and authorised to perform. If a task is outside your scope (e.g. wound dressings, medication administration you're not signed off for), stop and escalate — do not attempt it because you've "seen it done before."

Restrictive Practices

Any practice that restricts a resident's rights or freedom of movement (physical restraint, chemical restraint, locked doors, bed rails used to confine) is heavily regulated. PCAs never independently decide to use a restrictive practice — this requires authorisation, consent, and a behaviour support plan.

🚨 Balancing duty of care and dignity of risk

These two principles sit in tension deliberately. Duty of care doesn't mean wrapping residents in cotton wool, and dignity of risk doesn't mean ignoring genuine danger. When they conflict — flag it to the RN or team leader rather than deciding alone.

Working within a team

You work under the direction of registered staff and allied health. Follow the care plan as written; if it seems wrong, outdated, or unsafe for the resident's current condition, say so to the RN/EN rather than quietly deviating from it or quietly following something you believe is unsafe.

📋 Aged Care Quality Standards & Code of Conduct

Australian aged care providers, including facilities ACP works in, are regulated under the Aged Care Quality Standards and monitored by the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission. As a PCA, your day-to-day work is assessed against these standards, whether or not you ever see the paperwork.

Code of Conduct (Aged Care Code of Conduct)

All aged care workers — including PCAs — are individually bound by the Code of Conduct. Breaches can affect your registration with the NDIS Worker Screening/Aged Care Worker Screening database, not just your employment.

  • Act with respect for individual rights, dignity, and privacy
  • Provide care and services safely and competently, within scope
  • Act with integrity, honesty, and transparency
  • Report and respond to suspected abuse, neglect or unsafe practice
  • Never engage in sexual, physical, or emotional misconduct, or exploit a person you support financially or otherwise
  • Never provide care or services impaired by drugs or alcohol
⚠ Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS)

Certain incidents in residential aged care must be reported by the provider to the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission — including unexplained absence, unreasonable use of force, neglect, psychological/emotional abuse, and unexpected death. As a PCA, your job is to report what you see or suspect to your supervisor/RN immediately; they are responsible for the formal SIRS notification. You don't need to know the SIRS categories by heart, but you do need to escalate fast.

🤝 Person-Centred & Culturally Safe Care

Person-centred care means the resident's preferences, history, and goals shape how care is delivered — not just the task list. Two residents with identical care needs may want very different approaches.

In practice

  • Ask how someone likes things done, even for routine tasks — a shower order, a preferred side to be approached from, morning vs evening bathing
  • Explain what you're about to do before you do it, and check for consent each time — consent isn't a one-off event
  • Preserve privacy and dignity: close doors/curtains, cover the body appropriately, knock and wait
  • Recognise that behaviour changes (agitation, resistance to care) are often communication — pain, fear, confusion, or an unmet need — not "difficult" behaviour

Cultural safety

Residents come from a wide range of cultural, linguistic, and religious backgrounds, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, CALD communities, and LGBTIQ+ elders who may have experienced past discrimination in care settings. Cultural safety means the resident — not you — defines what feels safe and respectful.

  • Use interpreters for important conversations rather than family members where possible, especially for consent
  • Don't assume dietary, religious, or modesty preferences — check the care plan and ask
  • Use a person's correct name, pronouns, and title

🚶 Falls Prevention

Why this matters

Falls are the most common preventable harm event in aged care. Most falls have multiple contributing factors, and PCAs are often best placed to catch the early warning signs — this section is a PCA-level overview; ACP's full Falls Prevention module covers assessment and clinical strategy in depth.

Common contributing factors

  • Muscle weakness, poor balance, or a recent decline in mobility
  • Medication side effects (sedation, blood pressure drops)
  • Environmental hazards — clutter, poor lighting, wet floors, loose rugs
  • Incorrect or missing mobility aids (unlocked wheels, wrong-height frame)
  • Cognitive impairment or delirium affecting judgement of ability
  • Rushing — especially toileting urgency

ACP's approach to falls strategy

Long-termStrength and balance training, reviewed regularly by physiotherapy
Short-termCarer assistance, toileting schedules, environmental modification, sensor mats, medication review
Mobility aids4-wheeled walkers (4WW) are generally preferred over single sticks
Non-ambulant residentsLow-low beds and crash mats where indicated

What you do as a PCA

Follow the mobility care plan exactly

Use the aid and assistance level specified — don't upgrade or downgrade a resident's independence based on how they seem "today" without checking.

Keep the environment clear

Remove clutter, wipe spills immediately, ensure the call bell and mobility aid are within reach before you leave.

Respond to toileting needs promptly

Rushed, unassisted toileting attempts are one of the most common fall triggers — timely response reduces risk.

Report near misses, not just falls

A wobble, a "nearly went down," or a resident found on the floor unable to say what happened are all reportable.

If a fall happens

🚨 Do not move the resident

Do not attempt to lift or move a resident after a fall. Stay with them, call for help immediately (RN/EN or emergency services per your facility protocol), keep them warm and reassured, and do not give food or drink until they've been assessed — especially if head injury or anticoagulant use (e.g. warfarin) is possible.

🩷 Pressure Injury & Skin Integrity

Pressure injuries (also called pressure ulcers or bed sores) develop when sustained pressure — usually over a bony area — cuts off blood supply to the skin and underlying tissue. They can develop in hours and are largely preventable.

High-risk residents

  • Immobile or bed/chair-bound residents, or those who need help to reposition
  • Incontinent residents (moisture breaks down skin integrity)
  • Residents with poor nutrition/hydration or very thin, fragile skin
  • Residents with reduced sensation (e.g. diabetes, stroke) who may not feel warning discomfort

High-risk sites

Sacrum/tailbone, heels, hips, elbows, shoulder blades, ears, and the back of the head — anywhere bone sits close to skin.

Your role in prevention

Reposition on schedule

Follow the repositioning frequency in the care plan (commonly 2-hourly, but this varies by resident) — don't wait for a resident to ask.

Check skin every time you provide personal care

Look for redness that doesn't fade when pressed, discolouration, warmth, or broken skin — report anything new immediately, don't wait for the next scheduled skin check.

Keep skin clean and dry

Respond promptly to incontinence, pat rather than rub skin dry, and use barrier creams as directed in the care plan.

Use pressure-relieving equipment correctly

Air mattresses, heel protectors, and cushions only work if used properly — check they're switched on, correctly inflated, and positioned as intended.

Support nutrition and hydration

Encourage meals and fluids as able — poor nutrition significantly increases pressure injury risk. Report reduced intake to the RN.

⚠ Never do this

Never massage a reddened area over a bony prominence — this can worsen tissue damage. Never use a ring/donut cushion — it concentrates pressure at the edges. Report, don't self-treat.

🏃 Manual Handling Basics

Scope of this section

This is a general awareness overview. You must complete facility-specific, competency-based, supervised manual handling training and sign-off before using any hoist or transfer equipment — a verbal explanation alone is never sufficient.

Core principles

  • Always check the resident's individual manual handling plan before assisting — techniques are resident-specific, not one-size-fits-all
  • Use equipment (hoists, slide sheets, transfer belts) as trained — never improvise or skip a step to save time
  • Never attempt a two-person transfer alone, even if you think you can manage
  • Keep loads close to your body, bend at the knees/hips not the back, and avoid twisting while lifting
  • If a resident's condition changes (e.g. new pain, reduced weight-bearing), stop and escalate before continuing with the usual technique
🚨 If in doubt, stop

An unsafe transfer risks injury to both the resident and you. If equipment seems faulty, if you haven't been signed off on a specific technique, or if a second person isn't available when the plan requires one — stop and get help rather than proceeding.

🦼 Infection Prevention & Control

Standard precautions (apply to every resident, every time)

  • Hand hygiene before and after every episode of care — alcohol-based rub or wash with soap and water for 20+ seconds
  • Personal protective equipment (gloves, apron/gown, mask/eyewear) matched to the task
  • Safe handling and disposal of sharps, linen, and clinical waste per facility protocol
  • Respiratory hygiene/cough etiquette, and staying home when unwell yourself

Transmission-based precautions

Some residents require additional precautions (contact, droplet, or airborne) for infections such as gastroenteritis outbreaks, influenza, COVID-19, or multi-resistant organisms. Follow the signage on the resident's door and the facility's outbreak management protocol exactly — this protects other residents, not just you.

⚠ Report, don't guess

New diarrhoea, vomiting, fever, cough, or a wound that looks infected should be reported to the RN promptly — early reporting is what prevents outbreaks in a facility setting.

🛡️ Risk & Hazard Management

Risk management isn't a separate task from your shift — it's a habit of noticing. A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm; a risk is the likelihood and severity of that harm actually occurring.

Categories of hazard to watch for

CategoryExamples
EnvironmentalWet floors, trailing cords, poor lighting, clutter, broken furniture
EquipmentFaulty hoists/wheelchairs, flat batteries, unlocked bed wheels, damaged mattresses
ClinicalUndetected skin changes, medication errors, unmanaged pain, choking risk in meals
Behavioural/securityUnattended exits, wandering risk, aggression, unsupervised hazardous items
Manual handlingMissing second staff member, incorrect equipment for a resident's weight/needs

The hierarchy of risk control

When a hazard is identified, the most effective response is to eliminate it entirely; where that's not possible, reduce exposure (e.g. signage, rerouting, isolating the hazard); as a last resort, rely on individual precautions such as PPE. As a PCA, you rarely choose the control yourself — but reporting promptly is what allows the right control to be applied.

I check my work area for hazards at the start of every shift
I fix what I safely can myself (e.g. wipe a spill) and report what I can't
I never keep using equipment I suspect is faulty "just this once"
I report hazards even if no one was harmed this time

📝 Incident & Hazard Reporting

What must be reported

  • Incidents: falls, skin tears, pressure injuries, medication errors, choking events, aggression/behavioural incidents, missing resident, any injury to a resident, visitor, or staff member
  • Near misses: anything that could have caused harm but didn't this time — these are just as valuable to report because they reveal risk before someone is hurt
  • Hazards: anything you notice that could cause future harm, even without an incident attached to it
  • Suspected abuse or neglect: always reportable — see Safeguarding section
There is no such thing as "too small to report"

Under-reporting is one of the most common and most dangerous failures in aged care. A pattern only becomes visible if every instance is logged — a single unreported near-miss might be the one piece of data that would have prevented a serious harm event.

How to report well

Ensure immediate safety first

Attend to the resident's safety and wellbeing before documentation — call for help if needed.

Report verbally to the RN/team leader immediately

Don't wait until end of shift for anything involving actual or potential harm.

Complete the written incident report promptly

Use objective, factual language — what you saw and did, not assumptions about cause. "Resident found on floor beside bed, alert, states no pain" not "resident fell out of bed."

Stick to facts, not blame

Incident reports are about understanding what happened and preventing recurrence — not assigning fault. Avoid speculation about why something happened unless you witnessed it directly.

Follow up as directed

You may be asked for more detail as part of an investigation or root cause review — cooperate fully and honestly.

🚨 Never alter or delay a report

Never edit, backdate, or omit details from an incident report — including your own errors. Honest, timely reporting of your own mistakes is protected and expected; concealment is a serious breach of the Code of Conduct and can itself be a reportable matter.

🔒 Elder Abuse & Safeguarding

Elder abuse can be physical, psychological/emotional, financial, sexual, or neglect — and it can come from staff, other residents, family members, or visitors. PCAs are often best placed to notice early signs because of the time spent with residents.

Physical abuse

Unexplained bruising, fractures, or injuries; fear of a particular person; flinching at approach.

Psychological/emotional abuse

Withdrawal, anxiety, fearfulness, being talked over or dismissed in front of others, humiliation.

Financial abuse

Missing money or belongings, sudden changes to wills/power of attorney, a family member controlling all finances without the resident's involvement.

Neglect

Poor hygiene, unmet basic needs, untreated medical issues, malnutrition or dehydration not explained by clinical condition.

Sexual abuse

Any non-consensual sexual contact or behaviour — always a critical, immediate report regardless of the resident's cognitive status.

🚨 If you suspect abuse or neglect

Report immediately to your supervisor/RN — do not wait to be "sure." You are not required to investigate or gather proof; that is not your role. If the immediate risk involves a staff member and you're not comfortable reporting to your direct supervisor, escalate to a more senior manager or use your organisation's whistleblower/external reporting channel. If a resident is in immediate danger, call emergency services.

Mandatory reporting obligations vary by role and jurisdiction, but as a general principle: reasonable suspicion is enough to report. You will not be penalised for a good-faith report that turns out to be unfounded.

📁 Documentation & Privacy

Why documentation matters

If it isn't written down, it's treated as if it didn't happen. Accurate documentation protects residents, protects you, and is often the only record other staff have of what occurred on your shift.

  • Record care as it's delivered — not from memory at the end of the shift
  • Be factual and specific: times, observations, what was said, what was done
  • Never document care you didn't actually provide
  • Use approved abbreviations only; write legibly if handwritten

Privacy & confidentiality

  • Resident information is confidential — only share it with those who need it for care, never with other residents, unauthorised family, or on social media
  • Keep records, screens, and paperwork out of view of visitors and other residents
  • Photos of residents require explicit consent and must follow facility policy — never take personal photos of residents on a private device
⚠ Social media

Never post about residents, colleagues, or workplace incidents — even without names, details can be identifying, and this is a serious Code of Conduct breach.
