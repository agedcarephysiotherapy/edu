🎓 Course Overview

📋 Welcome, Support Worker

This comprehensive training course is designed specifically for NDIS support workers operating in community settings. You will learn how to safely monitor vital signs, recognise emergencies, respond appropriately, and safeguard the clients you support.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  1. Accurately measure and interpret the six key vital signs in adults with disability
  2. Recognise abnormal vital sign readings and know when to escalate
  3. Execute the DRABCD emergency action plan confidently
  4. Perform basic life support (CPR) and use an AED (not a replacement for an Accredited CPR or First Aid Training)
  5. Respond appropriately to common medical emergencies in community settings
  6. Apply safeguarding principles to protect client dignity and safety
  7. Communicate effectively with emergency services using ISBAR
  8. Complete accurate incident reports and documentation

⚠️ Important Notice

This course supplements — but does not replace — formal First Aid (HLTAID011) and CPR (HLTAID009) certification. You must maintain current certifications to work as an NDIS support worker. Always follow your organisation's policies and procedures, which take precedence over this training.

Key Terms

NDIS (National Disability Insurance Scheme)

Australian government scheme providing funding and support to people with permanent and significant disability. As a support worker, you deliver funded supports under the NDIS Practice Standards.

Duty of Care

Your legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to clients. This includes monitoring health, acting on concerns, and maintaining safe environments.

Dignity of Risk

The right of people with disability to make choices and take risks. Your role is to support informed decision-making while minimising harm — not to eliminate all risk.

Person-Centred Practice

Placing the client at the centre of all decisions. Their preferences, goals, and cultural background guide how you deliver support, including during emergencies.

NDIS Context & Duty of Care

Your Role in the Community

As a community-based NDIS support worker, you often work alone in clients' homes, shared accommodations, or community venues. Unlike hospital settings, you may not have immediate clinical backup. This makes your ability to recognise deterioration and respond appropriately critical.

🏠 Community Setting Realities
  • You may be the only person present during an emergency
  • Clients may have complex health conditions, communication difficulties, or sensory impairments
  • Equipment and space may be limited
  • Emergency services may take longer to arrive in remote areas
  • Client consent and dignity must be maintained even in crisis

NDIS Practice Standards Relevant to This Training

StandardWhat It Means for You
Provider GovernanceYou must know your organisation's emergency procedures and escalation pathways
Participant's LifeYou must act to preserve life and prevent serious harm
Provision of SupportsSupports must be delivered safely, including health monitoring
Safe EnvironmentYou must identify and manage environmental risks
Incident ManagementAll incidents must be reported, recorded, and reviewed

Scope of Practice — What You Can and Cannot Do

✅ Within Your Scope
  • Monitor and record vital signs as per care plan
  • Recognise abnormal signs and escalate to supervisor/nurse
  • Administer basic first aid and CPR
  • Call emergency services (000)
  • Follow documented emergency action plans
  • Administer prescribed emergency medication (e.g., EpiPen, Glucagon) if trained and authorised
❌ Outside Your Scope
  • Diagnose medical conditions
  • Prescribe or adjust medications
  • Perform invasive procedures (unless specifically trained and delegated)
  • Ignore abnormal readings hoping they "will sort themselves out"
  • Share client information with unauthorised persons

Documentation Requirements

Every interaction involving vital signs or health concerns must be documented. In community settings, if you don't document it, it didn't happen.

  • Date and time of observation
  • Vital sign readings with units
  • Client's appearance and behaviour
  • Any symptoms reported by client
  • Actions taken
  • Who was notified and when
  • Your full name and signature

Understanding Vital Signs

Vital signs are measurements of the body's basic functions. Changes in vital signs are often the first indicator that a person's health is deteriorating. Early recognition allows for early intervention.

The Six Key Vital Signs

Vital SignWhat It MeasuresNormal Range (Adult)Tool Needed
Body TemperatureBody's ability to generate and dissipate heat36.1°C – 37.2°CThermometer
Pulse / Heart RateHeart beats per minute60 – 100 bpmFingers or pulse oximeter
Respiration RateBreaths per minute12 – 20 breaths/minObservation only
Blood PressureForce of blood against artery walls90/60 – 120/80 mmHgSphygmomanometer + stethoscope
Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂)Percentage of oxygen in blood95% – 100%Pulse oximeter
Blood GlucoseSugar level in blood4.0 – 7.8 mmol/LGlucose meter + test strips
⚠️ Baseline Variations

People with disability may have different baseline vital signs due to their condition, medications, or autonomic dysfunction. Always compare readings to the client's usual baseline, not just population norms. Check the care plan for individual parameters.

When to Take Vital Signs

  • As scheduled in the client's care plan
  • When the client reports feeling unwell
  • Before and after administering certain medications
  • After a fall, seizure, or other incident
  • When you notice a change in behaviour, consciousness, or appearance
  • Before transferring to hospital or clinic

General Principles for All Measurements

Ensure Privacy and Dignity

Explain what you are doing, why, and gain consent. Ensure the environment is private. Be mindful of cultural or gender preferences.

Check Equipment

Ensure devices are clean, charged, calibrated, and within expiry dates. Use the correct cuff size for BP, correct probe size for SpO₂.

Position the Client Correctly

Most vitals are taken with the client seated or lying comfortably. Blood pressure should be taken with the arm supported at heart level.

Allow Rest Period

Wait 5 minutes after activity, eating, drinking hot/cold beverages, or smoking before taking vitals. Anxiety can also affect readings.

Take Multiple Readings

If a reading is abnormal, wait 2-3 minutes and repeat. Use the same arm for blood pressure comparisons.

Document Immediately

Record all readings with date, time, and any relevant context. Never rely on memory.

🌡️ Body Temperature

Body temperature reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss. It is controlled by the hypothalamus in the brain.

Normal Ranges by Site

Measurement SiteNormal RangeNotes
Oral (mouth)35.8°C – 37.3°CWait 15 min after eating/drinking
Axillary (armpit)34.8°C – 36.5°CLess accurate; add ~0.5°C for core estimate
Tympanic (ear)35.8°C – 37.5°CFast; ensure proper probe placement
Temporal (forehead)35.8°C – 37.5°CNon-invasive; affected by sweat/environment
Rectal36.3°C – 37.8°CMost accurate core temp; rarely used in community

How to Take an Oral Temperature

Prepare

Wash hands. Check thermometer is clean and functional. Ensure client has not eaten, drunk, or smoked in the last 15 minutes.

Explain and Position

Tell the client what you are doing. Ask them to sit comfortably. Place the probe under the tongue in the sublingual pocket.

Instruct the Client

Ask them to close lips gently around the probe (do not bite down) and breathe through the nose. Keep still until the thermometer beeps.

Read and Record

Remove the probe, read the display, and record the temperature with the site (e.g., "37.1°C oral"). Clean the probe with alcohol wipe.

Interpreting Temperature Readings

ReadingClassificationAction
Below 35.0°CHypothermiaURGENT: Warm gradually, call supervisor, consider 000 if severe
35.0°C – 36.0°CLow / Mild hypothermiaMonitor closely, re-check in 30 min, notify supervisor
36.1°C – 37.2°CNormalContinue routine monitoring
37.3°C – 38.0°CLow-grade feverMonitor, increase fluid intake, notify supervisor if persistent
38.1°C – 39.0°CModerate feverNotify supervisor immediately; may need GP review
Above 39.0°CHigh feverURGENT: Call supervisor; consider 000 if accompanied by confusion, stiff neck, or rash
🚨 Febrile Seizure Risk

Rapid temperature rise can trigger seizures in some clients (especially those with epilepsy or certain neurological conditions). If a client has a seizure, follow seizure first aid and call 000.

💓 Pulse & Heart Rate

The pulse reflects how many times the heart beats per minute (bpm). It indicates cardiovascular function and how well blood is circulating oxygen and nutrients.

Pulse Sites

SiteLocationBest Used For
RadialWrist, thumb sideRoutine monitoring; easiest for beginners
CarotidNeck, either side of windpipeEmergency pulse check; CPR assessment
BrachialInner elbowInfants; blood pressure measurement
ApicalChest, 5th intercostal spaceMost accurate; irregular rhythms

How to Take a Radial Pulse

Position

Ask the client to sit or lie comfortably. Support their arm, palm facing up, wrist slightly extended.

Locate the Pulse

Place your index and middle fingers (never thumb — it has its own pulse) on the thumb side of the wrist, just below the base of the thumb. Press lightly until you feel the beat.

Count for 30 or 60 Seconds

If regular: count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2. If irregular: count for a full 60 seconds. Note the rhythm (regular/irregular) and strength (strong/weak/bounding).

Record

Document rate, rhythm, and strength. Example: "72 bpm, regular, strong."

Interpreting Heart Rate

RateTermPossible CausesAction
Below 60 bpmBradycardiaAthletic fitness, medications (beta-blockers), hypothyroidism, heart block, hypothermiaCheck if this is baseline. If new onset, or with dizziness/fainting → notify supervisor
60 – 100 bpmNormalNormal resting rateContinue routine monitoring
100 – 120 bpmMild TachycardiaAnxiety, pain, fever, mild dehydration, caffeineRe-check after rest. Notify supervisor if persistent or with other symptoms
120 – 150 bpmModerate TachycardiaInfection, significant dehydration, heart conditions, blood lossNotify supervisor immediately
Above 150 bpmSevere TachycardiaMedical emergency — shock, severe infection, cardiac eventCall 000 and begin first aid
⚠️ Special Considerations for Clients with Disability
  • Autonomic Dysreflexia (spinal cord injury T6+): Sudden severe headache, sweating, goosebumps, and HR changes. Medical emergency — call 000 immediately.
  • Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS): HR increases >30 bpm on standing. Take vitals lying and standing.
  • Down Syndrome: Higher risk of congenital heart defects. Baseline may differ.

🫁 Respiration Rate

Respiration rate is the number of breaths per minute. It is one of the most important early warning signs of deterioration — yet it is often the least accurately measured vital sign.

🚨 Critical Fact

Changes in respiratory rate are often the first sign of clinical deterioration. A rise from 18 to 24 breaths/min may indicate pneumonia, sepsis, or heart failure before other signs appear.

Normal Respiratory Rates

Age GroupNormal Range
Adult (12+ years)12 – 20 breaths/min
Older Adult (65+)12 – 28 breaths/min (may be slightly higher)

How to Count Respirations

Do Not Tell the Client

People unconsciously alter their breathing if they know it is being watched. After taking the pulse, keep your fingers on the wrist and watch the chest rise and fall.

Observe for 60 Seconds

Count one full breath as one rise AND fall of the chest. Count for a full minute — respiratory rate is often irregular and 30-second counts are unreliable.

Assess Quality

Note depth (shallow/normal/deep), rhythm (regular/irregular), and any sounds (wheezing, crackling, grunting, stridor).

Record

Document rate and quality. Example: "18 breaths/min, regular, normal depth, no abnormal sounds."

Abnormal Breathing Patterns

PatternDescriptionPossible CauseAction
TachypnoeaFast breathing (>20/min)Fever, infection, anxiety, pain, metabolic acidosisMonitor; notify supervisor if >24 or with other symptoms
BradypnoeaSlow breathing (<10/min)Drug overdose, head injury, hypothermia, sleep apnoeaNotify supervisor immediately; check consciousness
ApnoeaPauses in breathing >10 secondsSleep apnoea, opioid overdose, brain injuryCall 000 if awake; position on side; monitor
Cheyne-StokesCycles of deep then shallow breathing, then apnoeaHeart failure, brain injury, end-of-lifeUrgent medical review required
KussmaulDeep, rapid, laboured breathingDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), severe metabolic acidosisCall 000 — check blood glucose immediately
StridorHarsh, high-pitched sound on inhalationUpper airway obstruction (allergic reaction, foreign body)Call 000 immediately — potential airway emergency
💡 Pro Tip for Community Workers

If a client has a tracheostomy or uses non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP/CPAP), you must still monitor their respiratory rate and effort. Check the care plan for specific parameters and emergency protocols.

🩺 Blood Pressure

Blood pressure (BP) measures the force of blood against artery walls. It is recorded as two numbers: systolic (heart contracting) over diastolic (heart relaxing).

Understanding the Numbers

CategorySystolic (mmHg)Diastolic (mmHg)Action
HypotensionBelow 90Below 60Monitor for dizziness; notify supervisor if symptomatic
Normal90 – 12060 – 80Continue routine monitoring
Elevated120 – 129Below 80Monitor; lifestyle factors
Stage 1 Hypertension130 – 13980 – 89Notify supervisor; may need GP review
Stage 2 Hypertension140 or above90 or aboveNotify supervisor immediately
Hypertensive CrisisAbove 180Above 120Call 000 — risk of stroke/heart attack

How to Measure Blood Pressure (Manual)

Prepare the Client

Ensure they have rested for 5 minutes. No caffeine, exercise, or smoking in the last 30 minutes. Ask them to empty their bladder.

Position Correctly

Client seated with back supported, feet flat on floor, legs uncrossed. Arm supported at heart level, palm facing up. Ensure cuff will go on bare arm (remove tight clothing).

Apply the Cuff

Wrap cuff snugly around upper arm, 2-3 cm above the elbow crease. The bladder should be centred over the brachial artery. Cuff width should cover 80% of arm circumference.

Locate the Brachial Pulse

Palpate the brachial artery in the antecubital fossa (inner elbow). Place the stethoscope diaphragm over this point — do not tuck it under the cuff.

Inflate the Cuff

Close the valve and inflate rapidly to 180 mmHg (or 20-30 mmHg above expected systolic). If you cannot palpate the pulse, inflate to 200 mmHg.

Deflate Slowly

Open the valve slightly and deflate at 2-3 mmHg per second. Listen for the first tapping sound (Korotkoff Phase 1) — this is the systolic pressure.

Record Diastolic

Continue deflating. The point where sounds disappear completely (Korotkoff Phase 5) is the diastolic pressure. If sounds do not disappear, note the muffling point (Phase 4).

Deflate Fully and Record

Deflate completely, remove cuff, and record both numbers with arm used and position. Wait 1-2 minutes before repeating on the other arm.

⚠️ Common Errors That Give False Readings
  • Cuff too small → falsely HIGH reading
  • Cuff too large → falsely LOW reading
  • Arm below heart level → falsely HIGH reading
  • Arm above heart level → falsely LOW reading
  • Legs crossed → falsely HIGH reading
  • Talking during measurement → falsely HIGH reading
  • Deflating too quickly → inaccurate reading

Using an Automated (Digital) BP Monitor

  1. Ensure batteries are charged and device is calibrated
  2. Follow positioning steps above
  3. Press start and remain still and silent
  4. Record the displayed reading
  5. If reading seems abnormal, wait 2 minutes and repeat
  6. Never rely solely on automated readings in emergencies — cross-check manually if trained

🔴 Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂)

Oxygen saturation measures the percentage of haemoglobin in the blood that is carrying oxygen. It is measured using a pulse oximeter — a small device that clips onto a finger, toe, or earlobe.

Normal SpO₂ Ranges

SpO₂ ReadingInterpretationAction
95% – 100%NormalContinue routine monitoring
90% – 94%Mild hypoxaemiaMonitor closely; notify supervisor; check for respiratory symptoms
85% – 89%Moderate hypoxaemiaUrgent: Notify supervisor immediately; prepare for oxygen if prescribed
Below 85%Severe hypoxaemiaCall 000 immediately — life-threatening

How to Use a Pulse Oximeter

Prepare

Ensure hands are warm. Remove nail polish or artificial nails from the finger you will use. Rest the client for 5 minutes.

Apply the Probe

Clip the oximeter onto a finger (index or middle finger preferred). Ensure the sensor is directly opposite the light emitter. The finger should be at heart level.

Wait for Reading

Keep the hand still. Wait for the waveform to stabilise and the reading to stop fluctuating (usually 10-30 seconds).

Record Both Numbers

Record SpO₂ % AND pulse rate. The oximeter displays both. If the pulse rate differs significantly from your manual count, re-check.

🚨 Factors That Give False SpO₂ Readings
  • Cold hands / poor circulation → falsely LOW or no reading
  • Nail polish / acrylic nails → falsely LOW
  • Movement / shivering → inaccurate reading
  • Bright ambient light → falsely HIGH
  • Anaemia → may read normal even if oxygen content is low
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning → falsely HIGH (oximeter cannot distinguish CO from O₂)

When SpO₂ Drops: Immediate Actions

Re-check the Reading

Ensure the probe is correctly positioned, the hand is warm and still, and there is no nail polish.

Assess the Client

Check colour (blue lips/fingertips = cyanosis), breathing effort, consciousness level, and ability to speak.

Position for Breathing

Sit the client upright (if conscious and not contraindicated). Loosen tight clothing around the neck and chest.

Administer Oxygen if Prescribed

If the client has prescribed oxygen (e.g., nasal prongs at 2L/min), apply it now. Do NOT adjust flow rates beyond what is prescribed without instruction.

Call for Help

If SpO₂ remains below 90% or the client is distressed: call 000. Notify your supervisor immediately.

🩸 Blood Glucose

Blood glucose monitoring is essential for clients with diabetes or those at risk of hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar). Both high and low glucose can be life-threatening.

Normal Blood Glucose Ranges (mmol/L)

ReadingClassificationAction
Below 4.0Hypoglycaemia (LOW)Treat immediately — see Hypoglycaemia protocol below
4.0 – 7.8Normal (fasting/random)Continue routine monitoring
7.9 – 11.0ElevatedMonitor; notify supervisor if persistent or with symptoms
11.1 – 15.0HighNotify supervisor; check for ketones if protocol requires
Above 15.0Very High / DKA RiskNotify supervisor immediately; check for ketones; call 000 if vomiting, confusion, or Kussmaul breathing

How to Measure Blood Glucose

Prepare Equipment

Wash hands thoroughly. Gather glucose meter, test strips (check expiry), lancet device, and sharps container. Calibrate meter if required.

Prepare the Site

Wash the client's hands with warm water (or use alcohol swab and let dry completely). Massage the finger gently to increase blood flow.

Perform the Finger Prick

Load the lancet. Prick the SIDE of the fingertip (less painful than the pad). Allow a drop of blood to form naturally — do not squeeze excessively.

Apply Blood to Test Strip

Touch the blood drop to the test strip (follow meter instructions — some require the strip to be in the meter first). Ensure enough blood fills the sample area.

Read and Record

Wait for the meter to display the result. Record the reading with date, time, and any symptoms. Dispose of lancet in sharps container immediately.

🚨 Hypoglycaemia Emergency — The "Rule of 15"

If blood glucose is below 4.0 mmol/L and the client is conscious and able to swallow:

  1. Give 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate immediately:
    • 3-4 glucose tablets, OR
    • 150ml fruit juice, OR
    • 1 tablespoon honey or sugar, OR
    • 5-6 jelly beans
  2. Wait 15 minutes, then re-check blood glucose
  3. If still below 4.0, repeat step 1
  4. Once above 4.0, give a snack with longer-acting carbohydrate (e.g., crackers, sandwich)

If unconscious, having a seizure, or unable to swallow: DO NOT give food or drink. Place in recovery position. Call 000 immediately. Administer Glucagon if trained and prescribed.

⚠️ Hyperglycaemia & DKA Warning Signs
  • Excessive thirst and urination
  • Fruity-smelling breath
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Abdominal pain
  • Confusion or drowsiness
  • Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid, laboured)

If any of these are present with high glucose: Call 000 immediately.

😣 Pain Assessment

Pain is considered the "fifth vital sign." Uncontrolled pain affects all other vital signs and significantly impacts quality of life. Many clients with disability may have difficulty communicating pain — your observational skills are critical.

Why Pain Matters

  • Pain increases heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate
  • Chronic pain leads to depression, anxiety, and social withdrawal
  • Untreated pain can mask other serious conditions
  • Some clients with intellectual disability may not localise or describe pain verbally

Pain Assessment Tools

ToolBest ForHow to Use
Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)Clients who can count and understand numbersAsk "On a scale of 0-10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable, what is your pain?"
Wong-Baker FACESChildren, clients with intellectual disability, or those with limited verbal skillsShow 6 faces ranging from smiling (0) to crying (10). Ask client to point to the face that matches their pain.
FLACC ScaleNon-verbal clients or those with severe communication impairmentScore 0-2 on each: Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability. Total 0-10. Used for clients who cannot self-report.
Abbey Pain ScaleClients with dementia or severe cognitive impairmentObserve vocalisation, facial expression, body language, behavioural changes, physiological changes, and physical changes. Score each 0-3.
PainAD ScaleClients with advanced dementiaScore breathing, negative vocalisation, facial expression, body language, and consolability. 0-2 each, total 0-10.

Behavioural Signs of Pain in Non-Verbal Clients

👀 Watch For These Signs
  • Facial expressions: Grimacing, frowning, clenched jaw, rapid blinking
  • Vocalisation: Moaning, groaning, crying, screaming, or unusually quiet
  • Body language: Guarding an area, rocking, pacing, or being very still
  • Changes in behaviour: Aggression, withdrawal, refusing activities they usually enjoy
  • Changes in vital signs: Increased HR, BP, RR, or temperature
  • Sleep disturbance: Difficulty falling or staying asleep
  • Appetite changes: Refusing food or drink

When to Escalate Pain Concerns

  • New onset pain (especially chest, abdominal, or severe headache)
  • Pain score increasing despite prescribed analgesia
  • Pain accompanied by fever, vomiting, or changes in consciousness
  • Any sudden, severe pain — always notify supervisor
💡 Remember

"If they could tell us, they would." For clients who cannot communicate verbally, assume pain is present if you observe behavioural changes, and act accordingly. Document your observations in detail.

Escalation Pathways

Green — Routine

All vital signs within normal range or client's usual baseline. Continue scheduled monitoring. Document in client file.

Yellow — Caution

One vital sign slightly abnormal but client appears well. Re-check in 15-30 minutes. Notify supervisor (phone call or message). Document and continue monitoring.

Orange — Urgent

Multiple vital signs abnormal OR one significantly abnormal OR client looks unwell. Call supervisor immediately. Stay with client. Prepare for possible 000 call. Document all actions.

Red — Emergency

Life-threatening vital signs OR unconsciousness OR severe distress. Call 000 immediately. Then notify supervisor. Begin first aid/CPR as needed. Do NOT leave client alone.

🚨 When in Doubt, Call It Out

It is always better to notify your supervisor and be told "that's fine, just monitor" than to NOT notify and have the client deteriorate. You are not expected to diagnose — you are expected to recognise and escalate.

🚨 Emergency Response Principles

Medical emergencies can happen at any time in community settings. Your calm, systematic response can save lives. Remember: you are not expected to be a paramedic — you are expected to recognise emergencies, keep the client safe, and get professional help.

The Golden Rules of Emergency Response

Stay Calm

Your calmness reassures the client and anyone else present. Take a deep breath. Panic clouds judgment.

Assess the Scene for Danger

Before approaching, check for hazards: traffic, fire, electrical risks, aggressive animals, or violent individuals. Do NOT become a second casualty.

Assess the Client

Check responsiveness, breathing, and circulation. Use the DRABCD action plan (next section).

Call for Help Early

If in doubt, call 000. It is better to call and cancel than to delay. In cardiac arrest, every minute without CPR reduces survival by 10%.

Do No Further Harm

Only perform actions you are trained and authorised to do. Do not move a client with suspected spinal injury unless absolutely necessary.

Document Everything

As soon as the emergency is stable, document times, actions, observations, and who was notified.

When to Call 000 Immediately

🚨 Call 000 NOW If You See:
  • Unconsciousness or unresponsiveness
  • Difficulty breathing or stopped breathing
  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Severe bleeding that won't stop
  • Signs of stroke (FAST: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call)
  • Seizure lasting more than 5 minutes or repeated seizures without recovery
  • Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)
  • Suspected spinal or head injury with altered consciousness
  • Drug or alcohol overdose
  • Severe burns (especially to face, hands, genitals, or larger than the person's palm)
  • Any situation where you feel "something is seriously wrong" — trust your instincts

NDIS-Specific Emergency Considerations

🏠 Community Realities
  • Communication barriers: Some clients may not be able to tell you what is wrong. Learn their baseline behaviour and non-verbal cues.
  • Mobility aids: Wheelchairs, hoists, and specialised equipment may complicate positioning or evacuation. Know how to use them safely.
  • Medication: Some clients take medications that mask symptoms (e.g., beta-blockers hide tachycardia). Be extra vigilant.
  • Behavioural support: A client in pain or distress may exhibit challenging behaviour. Stay calm and use de-escalation techniques.
  • Consent: In an emergency where the client cannot consent, you may provide necessary first aid under implied consent. Document this.

🆘 DRABCD Action Plan

DRABCD is the systematic approach to assessing and managing any unconscious or collapsed person. It is the foundation of all emergency first aid.

D — Danger
R — Response
A — Airway
B — Breathing
C — CPR
D — Defibrillation

D — Danger

Before approaching the casualty, scan the area for hazards:

  • Traffic, fire, smoke, electrical wires
  • Hazardous substances or gases
  • Hostile individuals or animals
  • Unsafe structures or surfaces

Do NOT put yourself at risk. If the scene is unsafe, call 000 and wait for emergency services.

R — Response

Check if the person is responsive:

Talk to Them

"Hello, can you hear me? Open your eyes." Speak loudly and clearly.

Touch Them

Gently squeeze their shoulders. For clients with sensory issues, be mindful of touch sensitivity.

Observe

Do they move, make sounds, or open their eyes?

If responsive: Ask what happened. Check for injuries. Monitor vital signs. Call 000 if needed.

If unresponsive: Call 000 immediately (or send someone to call). Place on their back on a firm surface and proceed to Airway.

A — Airway

An open airway is essential for breathing. An unconscious person's tongue can fall back and block the airway.

Open the Mouth

Look inside for obvious obstructions (food, vomit, dentures). Remove visible objects with gloved fingers — do NOT do blind finger sweeps.

Tilt Head, Lift Chin

Place one hand on the forehead and two fingers under the chin. Gently tilt the head back and lift the chin. For infants, use only a slight head tilt.

Jaw Thrust (if spinal injury suspected)

If you suspect neck/spinal injury, do NOT tilt the head. Instead, place fingers behind the jaw angles and thrust forward to open the airway without moving the neck.

⚠️ Caution

If the client has a tracheostomy, check that the tube is patent (not blocked or dislodged). Suction if trained and equipment is available. Call 000 immediately.

B — Breathing

Look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for up to 10 seconds:

  • LOOK for rise and fall of the chest
  • LISTEN for breath sounds at the mouth/nose
  • FEEL for air movement on your cheek

Gasping or occasional breaths are NOT normal breathing. If the person is not breathing normally, start CPR immediately.

If breathing normally: Place in the recovery position (see below), monitor breathing, and wait for emergency services.

C — CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation)

If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR immediately. See the CPR & AED module for detailed instructions.

D — Defibrillation

If an AED (Automated External Defibrillator) is available, apply it as soon as possible and follow the voice prompts. See the CPR & AED module.

The Recovery Position

Use the recovery position for an unconscious person who IS breathing normally. It keeps the airway open and prevents aspiration of vomit.

Kneel Beside the Person

Ensure they are on their back on a firm, flat surface.

Position the Arm

Place the arm nearest to you at a right angle to their body, with the palm facing up.

Bend the Far Leg

Bring the far leg up at the knee so the foot is flat on the floor.

Roll Them Toward You

Support the head and neck with one hand. Grasp the far knee with your other hand and roll them toward you onto their side.

Position the Head

Tilt the head back slightly to ensure the airway remains open. Adjust the top leg so both hip and knee are bent at right angles.

Monitor

Stay with them, monitor breathing, and be prepared to roll them back and start CPR if breathing stops.

🚨 Do NOT Use Recovery Position If:
  • You suspect spinal injury (keep them still, support head and neck)
  • They have major injuries to the side you would roll them onto
  • They are in cardiac arrest (start CPR instead)

❤️ CPR & AED

Cardiac arrest means the heart has stopped pumping blood effectively. Without immediate CPR and defibrillation, the person will die. In Australia, survival rates drop by approximately 10% for every minute without CPR.

🚨 Cardiac Arrest vs Heart Attack

Cardiac arrest = The heart stops pumping. The person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. CPR needed immediately.

Heart attack = A blocked artery reduces blood flow to the heart. The person is usually conscious, with chest pain, sweating, and nausea. Call 000 immediately. A heart attack CAN lead to cardiac arrest.

Adult CPR — Step by Step

Ensure Safety and Call 000

Check for danger. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, call 000 immediately. Put the phone on speaker so you can talk to the operator while doing CPR.

Start Chest Compressions

Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest (lower half of the sternum/breastbone). Place your other hand on top and interlock fingers. Keep arms straight and shoulders directly over hands.

Push Hard and Fast

Compress the chest to a depth of approximately one-third of the chest depth (about 5-6 cm for adults). Rate: 100-120 compressions per minute. Allow the chest to recoil fully between compressions. Do NOT lean on the chest.

Give Rescue Breaths (if trained)

After 30 compressions, open the airway (head tilt, chin lift). Pinch the nose shut. Take a normal breath, seal your mouth over theirs, and blow steadily for about 1 second until the chest rises. Give 2 breaths. If the chest does not rise, re-tilt the head and try again.

Continue Cycles

Continue 30 compressions : 2 breaths. If you are unwilling or unable to give rescue breaths, do compression-only CPR. Do not stop until: an AED is ready, the person shows signs of life, emergency services take over, or you are physically exhausted.

🎵 Compression Timing Tip

Push to the beat of "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees (approximately 100-120 bpm). This helps maintain the correct rate.

Using an AED (Automated External Defibrillator)

Turn On the AED

Press the power button. Follow the voice prompts.

Expose the Chest

Remove clothing from the chest. If the chest is wet, dry it. If there is excessive chest hair, quickly shave or use a razor (some AED kits include one). Do NOT delay CPR for this.

Apply the Pads

Peel off backing and stick pads firmly to bare skin:

  • One pad on the upper right chest, below the collarbone
  • One pad on the lower left side, below the armpit
If pads touch (e.g., small child), place one on the front and one on the back.

Analyse Rhythm

The AED will say "Analysing — do not touch the patient." Ensure no one is touching the person. If a shock is advised, the AED will charge and say "Stand clear."

Deliver Shock

Ensure everyone is clear (loudly say "Stand clear!"). Press the shock button when prompted. Some AEDs deliver the shock automatically.

Resume CPR

Immediately resume CPR for 2 minutes (about 5 cycles of 30:2). The AED will re-analyse after 2 minutes. Continue until emergency services arrive or the person shows signs of life.

⚠️ AED Safety
  • Do NOT use an AED in standing water. Move the person to a dry area if safe.
  • Remove medication patches from the chest before applying pads.
  • Do NOT place pads over implanted devices (pacemaker/ICD) — place at least 8cm away.
  • Do NOT touch the person during analysis or shock delivery.

Special Considerations for Clients with Disability

  • Obesity: You may need to push deeper. Do not be afraid to push hard — broken ribs can heal; brain death cannot.
  • Pregnancy: If the woman is visibly pregnant and on her back, place a firm object under her right hip to tilt her slightly to the left. This prevents the uterus from compressing major blood vessels.
  • Frail/osteoporotic clients: Push as hard as needed for effective compressions. Fractures may occur but are acceptable given the alternative.
  • Clients with tracheostomies: If the tracheostomy tube is patent, you can give rescue breaths through the tube using a bag-valve mask if trained. Otherwise, seal your mouth over the stoma and breathe.

😮 Choking Response

Choking occurs when the airway is partially or completely blocked by a foreign object (usually food). It is a medical emergency that can rapidly become fatal.

Recognising Choking

SignMild Obstruction (Can Cough)Severe Obstruction (Cannot Cough)
CoughingEffective, forceful coughWeak or absent cough; silent
BreathingSome air movementNo air movement; high-pitched noise or silent
Skin colourNormal or slightly flushedBlue lips, face, or fingertips (cyanosis)
ConsciousnessAlert, distressedMay become unconscious quickly
Your ActionEncourage coughing. Do NOT slap back.Back blows + chest thrusts. Call 000.

Adult Choking First Aid — Severe Obstruction

Call 000

If someone else is present, send them to call 000 immediately. If alone, call 000 on speakerphone before starting first aid.

Give 5 Back Blows

Stand slightly behind and to the side. Support the chest with one hand. Lean the person forward. Give 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.

Give 5 Chest Thrusts

Stand behind the person. Place one hand in the centre of their chest (same position as CPR). Give 5 sharp inward thrusts. Each thrust should be separate and distinct.

Alternate

Continue alternating 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unconscious.

If the Person Becomes Unconscious

🚨 Start CPR Immediately
  1. Lower them carefully to the ground
  2. Call 000 (if not already done)
  3. Begin CPR — the chest compressions may dislodge the object
  4. Each time you open the airway to give breaths, look in the mouth and remove any visible object
  5. Do NOT do blind finger sweeps

Special Considerations for Clients with Disability

  • Dysphagia (swallowing difficulties): Common in cerebral palsy, stroke, and some neurological conditions. These clients are at higher risk of choking. Know their texture-modified diet requirements and supervise eating.
  • Wheelchair users: If the person cannot lean forward, support them as best you can. Chest thrusts can be performed while they are seated.
  • Clients with limited neck mobility: Be cautious with positioning. Do NOT force the head into positions that cause pain or injury.
  • Pica (eating non-food items): Some clients with intellectual disability may ingest foreign objects. Be vigilant and remove hazardous items from the environment.

⚡ Seizure Management

Seizures are common in people with epilepsy, brain injury, or certain genetic conditions. Most seizures are self-limiting, but some require emergency intervention.

Types of Seizures

First Aid for Tonic-Clonic Seizures

Stay Calm and Time It

Note the start time. Most tonic-clonic seizures last 1-3 minutes.

Protect from Injury

Clear nearby objects (furniture, sharp items). Place something soft under their head (jacket, pillow). Do NOT restrain their movements or try to hold them down.

Do NOT Put Anything in Their Mouth

They cannot swallow their tongue. Putting objects in the mouth can break teeth, cause aspiration, or injure you.

Roll Onto Side (If Safe)

Once the jerking stops and they are breathing, gently roll them onto their side (recovery position) to keep the airway clear. This also helps saliva or vomit drain.

Stay With Them

They may be confused, sleepy, or agitated for minutes to hours (post-ictal state). Reassure them calmly. Do NOT give food or drink until fully alert.

Call 000 If:
  • Seizure lasts more than 5 minutes
  • They have repeated seizures without recovering
  • They are injured during the seizure
  • They have difficulty breathing after the seizure
  • This is their first known seizure
  • They are pregnant or have diabetes
  • The seizure occurs in water
💡 After a Seizure (Post-Ictal Phase)
  • The person may be confused, sleepy, or have a headache
  • They may not remember the seizure
  • They may be embarrassed or emotional
  • Offer reassurance in a calm, quiet environment
  • Do NOT leave them alone until fully recovered
  • Document the seizure: type, duration, body parts involved, incontinence, injuries, recovery time

Rescue Medication for Seizures

Some clients have prescribed emergency seizure medication (e.g., Midazolam). You must be specifically trained and authorised to administer this. Check the care plan.

✅ If Administering Midazolam (Buccal or Intranasal)
  1. Call 000 FIRST
  2. Check the care plan and medication chart
  3. Confirm the dose and expiry date
  4. Administer as trained (usually between gum and cheek, or into the nose)
  5. Time the seizure — if it continues 5 minutes after medication, a second dose may be given per care plan
  6. Monitor breathing and airway. Be prepared to give rescue breaths if breathing stops

🦴 Falls & Head Injuries

Falls are one of the most common incidents in community disability support. While many falls result in minor injuries, some can cause serious harm — especially head injuries in clients taking anticoagulant medications (blood thinners).

Immediate Response to a Fall

Stay Calm and Approach

Do NOT rush to lift the person. First, check for hazards (broken glass, electrical cords, wet floors).

Assess Responsiveness

Talk to them: "Are you okay? Can you hear me?" Check for responsiveness, breathing, and severe bleeding.

Do NOT Move Them (Initially)

If they are unconscious, have severe pain, or you suspect spinal injury, keep them still and call 000. Moving them could worsen injuries.

Check for Injuries

Look for visible injuries: bleeding, deformities, swelling, bruising. Ask where it hurts. Check for head injuries especially.

If Conscious and No Serious Injury

Help them to a comfortable position. Use a hoist or assistive device if available and you are trained. Do NOT lift alone if they are heavy or you are unsure.

Take Vital Signs

Check temperature, pulse, respirations, BP, and SpO₂. Look for signs of shock (pale, clammy skin, rapid pulse, low BP).

Notify Supervisor

Report the fall immediately, even if there are no visible injuries. Some complications (like internal bleeding or concussion) may not appear immediately.

Head Injury — When to Call 000

🚨 Call 000 Immediately If:
  • Loss of consciousness (even brief)
  • Confusion, disorientation, or memory loss (cannot remember the fall)
  • Severe headache or worsening headache
  • Vomiting (especially more than once)
  • Seizure
  • Weakness, numbness, or tingling in limbs
  • Difficulty speaking, seeing, or walking
  • Blood or clear fluid leaking from ears or nose
  • Bruising around eyes or behind ears ("raccoon eyes" or Battle's sign)
  • Deep cut or laceration to the head
  • The person is taking blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) — even minor head bumps can cause serious bleeding

Concussion Signs and Symptoms

PhysicalCognitiveEmotionalSleep
Headache, dizziness, nausea, balance problems, sensitivity to light/noise, blurred vision, fatigueConfusion, memory problems, difficulty concentrating, feeling "foggy", slowed responsesIrritability, sadness, nervousness, emotional changesDrowsiness, sleeping more or less than usual, difficulty falling asleep

If concussion is suspected: The person must be medically assessed. Do NOT let them sleep for the first few hours after a head injury unless a doctor has assessed them. Monitor closely for deteriorating symptoms.

Preventing Falls

  • Keep walkways clear of clutter, cords, and loose rugs
  • Ensure adequate lighting, especially at night
  • Install grab bars in bathrooms
  • Use non-slip mats in showers and baths
  • Ensure footwear fits well and has non-slip soles
  • Review medications that may cause dizziness or drowsiness
  • Use appropriate mobility aids (walkers, frames) and ensure they are maintained
  • Address vision and hearing issues promptly
  • Conduct regular home safety assessments

🐝 Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction that can occur within minutes of exposure to an allergen. It requires immediate treatment with adrenaline (epinephrine).

Recognising Anaphylaxis

🚨 Signs of Anaphylaxis (Any ONE of these is enough to treat)
  • Difficult or noisy breathing (wheezing, stridor, hoarse voice)
  • Swelling of tongue or throat
  • Tightness in throat or difficulty swallowing
  • Persistent cough or change in voice
  • Dizziness, collapse, or loss of consciousness
  • Pale and floppy (in younger clients)

PLUS any of these skin/mucosal symptoms:

  • Hives (urticaria) — raised, itchy welts
  • Swelling of lips, face, or eyes
  • Flushing or generalised redness
  • Abdominal pain, vomiting (especially after insect sting or food)

EpiPen Administration — Step by Step

Call 000 Immediately

Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency. Call 000 first, or have someone else call while you administer the EpiPen.

Hold the EpiPen Correctly

Form a fist around the EpiPen with the orange tip pointing down. Remove the blue safety cap by pulling straight up (do NOT bend or twist).

Inject Into Outer Thigh

Place the orange tip against the outer mid-thigh (through clothing if necessary — it will go through jeans). Push down firmly until you hear a click. Hold for 3 seconds (count "1, 2, 3").

Remove and Massage

Remove the EpiPen and massage the injection site for 10 seconds. The needle will be visible — be careful.

Note the Time

Record the time of administration. A second dose may be needed after 5 minutes if symptoms persist or worsen.

Position the Person

If breathing is difficult: sit them up. If dizzy or collapsed: lie them flat with legs elevated. If pregnant: lie on left side. Do NOT stand or walk them.

Monitor and Wait

Stay with them. Monitor breathing and consciousness. Be prepared to give CPR if they stop breathing. Give a second EpiPen after 5 minutes if no improvement and ambulance not yet arrived.

⚠️ Important EpiPen Notes
  • Do NOT inject into buttocks, hands, or feet — thigh only
  • Do NOT hesitate to use the EpiPen — it is safe and necessary in anaphylaxis
  • Even if symptoms improve after EpiPen, the person MUST still go to hospital — biphasic reactions can occur hours later
  • Check expiry dates regularly and store EpiPens at room temperature (not in cars or fridges)
  • Always carry two EpiPens if prescribed

Common Triggers in Community Settings

  • Foods: Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk, wheat, sesame, soy
  • Insect stings: Bees, wasps, ants (especially jack jumper ants in Australia)
  • Medications: Penicillin, aspirin, NSAIDs, anaesthetics
  • Latex: Gloves, balloons, elastic bands

🍬 Diabetic Emergency

Clients with diabetes can experience two main emergencies: hypoglycaemia (blood sugar too low) and hyperglycaemia/DKA (blood sugar too high). Both can be life-threatening.

Hypoglycaemia (LOW Blood Sugar) — The Most Common Emergency

🚨 Recognise Hypoglycaemia ("Hypo")

Blood glucose below 4.0 mmol/L

Symptoms: Shaking, sweating, hunger, dizziness, confusion, irritability, weakness, headache, blurred vision, pale skin, rapid heartbeat. Severe: seizures, unconsciousness.

Treating Hypoglycaemia — The Rule of 15

Check Blood Glucose

If the client has symptoms of a hypo, check blood glucose immediately. If below 4.0 mmol/L, treat.

Give 15g Fast-Acting Carbohydrate

Choose ONE of:

  • 3-4 glucose tablets
  • 150ml fruit juice or regular soft drink (NOT diet)
  • 1 tablespoon honey or sugar
  • 5-6 jelly beans

Wait 15 Minutes

Stay with the client. Keep them safe. Do NOT give insulin or exercise.

Re-check Blood Glucose

If still below 4.0, repeat step 2. If above 4.0, proceed to step 5.

Give Longer-Acting Carbohydrate

Once blood glucose is above 4.0, give a snack with complex carbs and protein to prevent another drop:

  • Crackers with cheese or peanut butter
  • Sandwich
  • Glass of milk and a piece of fruit

🚨 Severe Hypoglycaemia — Unconscious or Seizing
  1. Call 000 immediately
  2. Place in recovery position
  3. Do NOT give food or drink (risk of choking/aspiration)
  4. Administer Glucagon injection if trained, prescribed, and available (usually into thigh or buttock muscle)
  5. Monitor breathing. Be prepared to give rescue breaths if breathing stops
  6. Once conscious and able to swallow, give fast-acting carbohydrate

Hyperglycaemia & Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

⚠️ Recognise DKA — Medical Emergency

Blood glucose usually above 15.0 mmol/L with ketones present

Symptoms: Excessive thirst and urination, fruity-smelling breath, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, deep rapid breathing (Kussmaul), confusion, drowsiness, weakness.

Action: Call 000 immediately. Give water if conscious and able to swallow. Do NOT give insulin unless you are specifically trained and authorised.

Preventing Diabetic Emergencies

  • Ensure meals are on time and balanced
  • Monitor blood glucose as per care plan
  • Recognise early signs of highs and lows
  • Ensure fast-acting carbs are always accessible
  • Know the client's insulin/medication schedule
  • Be aware that illness, stress, and infection can affect blood glucose

📝 Incident Reporting

Under the NDIS, all reportable incidents must be recorded and reported to the NDIS Commission. Understanding what constitutes a reportable incident and how to document it is essential.

What is a Reportable Incident?

🚨 NDIS Reportable Incidents Include:
  • Death of a participant
  • Serious injury to a participant
  • Abuse or neglect of a participant
  • Unlawful sexual or physical contact with a participant
  • Sexual misconduct committed against, or in the presence of, a participant
  • Use of a restrictive practice without authorisation
  • Use of a prohibited restrictive practice

Writing an Effective Incident Report

Write Objectively

Use facts, not opinions. "Client fell while transferring from wheelchair to bed" — NOT "Client was clumsy and fell."

Be Specific

Include exact times, measurements, and descriptions. "BP 180/110 mmHg" — NOT "BP was high."

Use the Client's Words

If the client said something relevant, put it in quotation marks. "Client stated: 'I felt dizzy before I fell.'"

Include All Actions Taken

What first aid was given? Who was notified? What time? What was their response?

Note Witnesses

Record names and contact details of anyone who saw the incident.

Sign and Date

Every report must be signed and dated. Electronic signatures are acceptable if your system uses them.

📞 Contacting Emergency Services

Calling 000 is a critical skill. The information you provide determines how quickly and appropriately emergency services respond.

What Happens When You Call 000

You Will Be Asked: "Emergency. Police, Fire, or Ambulance?"

State clearly: "Ambulance" (or Police/Fire if applicable). You may be transferred.

Provide Your Location FIRST

This is the most important piece of information. Give the exact address, including unit number, building name, and any access instructions (e.g., "Gate code 1234", "Use side entrance").

Describe the Emergency

Be concise and factual. "My client is unconscious and not breathing. I am doing CPR." OR "My client is having a seizure that has lasted 7 minutes."

Answer Questions

The operator will ask follow-up questions. Stay on the line. Do NOT hang up until told to do so. The operator can guide you through first aid procedures.

Follow Instructions

The operator may give you specific instructions (e.g., "Continue CPR", "Do NOT move them", "Unlock the front door"). Follow them exactly.

Send Someone to Meet the Ambulance

If possible, have another person wait at the entrance to guide paramedics. Turn on outdoor lights if it is dark.

Key Information to Have Ready

  • Exact address and location details
  • Client's full name and date of birth
  • Client's known medical conditions and allergies
  • Current medications (especially blood thinners, insulin, epilepsy meds)
  • Emergency contact details
  • NDIS provider name and contact
  • Any advance care directives or resuscitation plans
  • Access information (gate codes, parking, stairs/elevator)

Non-Urgent Medical Advice: HealthDirect

📱 1800 022 222 (HealthDirect)

For non-life-threatening concerns where you are unsure whether to call an ambulance. A registered nurse will assess the situation and advise. Available 24/7.

NDIS Incident Management System

After calling 000 and stabilising the client, you must also report the incident through your organisation's incident management system and notify the NDIS Commission if it is a reportable incident.

🎭 Practical Scenarios

Test your knowledge with these realistic community-based scenarios. Read each scenario, decide your actions, then reveal the answer.

Scenario 1: The Quiet Morning

You arrive at 08:00 to support Tom, a 45-year-old man with Down syndrome and Type 2 diabetes. He is usually cheerful and chatty, but today he is very quiet, pale, and says he feels "funny." You check his blood glucose: 3.2 mmol/L. He is conscious and can swallow.

What do you do?

Answer: Tom is having a hypoglycaemic episode (blood glucose below 4.0 mmol/L). Follow the Rule of 15:

  1. Give 15g fast-acting carbohydrate immediately (e.g., 150ml fruit juice, 3-4 glucose tablets, or 1 tablespoon honey)
  2. Wait 15 minutes
  3. Re-check blood glucose
  4. If still below 4.0, repeat
  5. Once above 4.0, give a longer-acting snack (e.g., crackers with cheese)

Document the episode, actions taken, and notify your supervisor. Review Tom's meal timing and medication with the care team to prevent recurrence.

Scenario 2: The Fall in the Bathroom

You hear a thud from the bathroom. You find Maria, a 62-year-old woman with multiple sclerosis, on the floor next to her walker. She is conscious but confused and says her head hurts. There is a small amount of blood on the floor tiles. She takes warfarin for a heart condition.

What do you do?

Answer: This is a high-risk situation due to the head injury AND warfarin use (increased bleeding risk).

  1. Call 000 immediately — head injury + anticoagulant = high risk of intracranial bleeding
  2. Do NOT move Maria unless absolutely necessary (risk of spinal injury)
  3. Apply gentle pressure to any bleeding wound with a clean cloth
  4. Monitor consciousness, breathing, and vital signs
  5. Keep her warm and reassured
  6. Notify supervisor and emergency contact
  7. Document everything: time of fall, observations, actions, 000 call time

Scenario 3: The Seizure at Lunch

You are having lunch with David, a 28-year-old man with epilepsy. Suddenly his eyes roll back, his body goes stiff, and he falls sideways off his chair. He begins jerking violently. Lunch was pasta with tomato sauce. You start your watch: the jerking continues.

What do you do?

Answer: David is having a tonic-clonic seizure.

  1. Note the time the seizure started
  2. Clear nearby objects to prevent injury
  3. Place something soft under his head
  4. Do NOT restrain his movements
  5. Do NOT put anything in his mouth
  6. Turn him onto his side (recovery position) once jerking stops, if safe to do so
  7. If the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, or if he has repeated seizures without recovering: Call 000
  8. Check his care plan for prescribed emergency medication (e.g., Midazolam) and administer if trained and authorised
  9. Stay with him during the post-ictal phase. Do NOT give food or drink until fully alert
  10. Document: start time, duration, body parts involved, incontinence, injuries, recovery time

Scenario 4: The Allergic Reaction

Sarah, a 19-year-old woman with autism, eats a biscuit at a community event. Within 2 minutes, she starts coughing, her lips swell, and she develops hives on her neck. She points to her throat and looks frightened. You know she has a peanut allergy and carries an EpiPen in her bag.

What do you do?

Answer: Sarah is having anaphylaxis — a life-threatening allergic reaction.

  1. Call 000 immediately
  2. Locate her EpiPen and administer it into the outer mid-thigh (can go through clothing)
  3. Hold for 3 seconds, then remove
  4. Note the time of administration
  5. Lay her flat with legs elevated (or sit if breathing is difficult)
  6. Stay with her and monitor breathing and consciousness
  7. If no improvement after 5 minutes and ambulance not arrived, give a second EpiPen if available
  8. Even if she improves, she MUST go to hospital for observation (risk of biphasic reaction)
  9. Notify supervisor, family, and document everything

Scenario 5: The Unusual Bruising

During a shower, you notice several bruises on Peter's upper arms and thighs — areas not usually injured. The bruises are in various stages of healing. Peter, who has an intellectual disability, becomes very quiet when you ask about them and looks toward the door. His mother was the only other person with him this week.

What do you do?

Answer: This is a safeguarding concern requiring immediate action.

  1. Do NOT confront the mother or accuse anyone
  2. Document exactly what you observed: location, size, colour, and Peter's reaction. Take photos if your policy allows and Peter consents
  3. Report to your supervisor/manager immediately
  4. Your organisation will investigate and may report to the NDIS Commission and/or police
  5. Continue to support Peter normally. Do not let him know you have reported concerns
  6. Monitor for further injuries or behavioural changes
  7. Remember: you are not investigating — you are reporting observations in good faith
